How to use this guide, in sixty seconds

Three rules, and then the list.

There are one hundred and one items in here. You are not supposed to buy all of them. Nobody does. Here is how the people who actually stick with this use it.

Rule one. Start with what is already in your kitchen.

Walk to your pantry with this guide open. You will find five or six items on the list that you already own and never thought of as blood pressure food. Onions. Oats. A can of black beans. A jar of cinnamon. Start there. Nothing kills a health change faster than a shopping trip you cannot afford.

Not ten new foods today. One. Today spinach. Tomorrow garlic. The day after, oats. Ten days from now you will have ten new habits instead of one abandoned diet, and you will know which ones you actually like. There is a ten day starter near the back that does the choosing for you.

The idea this whole guide is built on

Three pressures. Three hormones. One story.

Blood pressure is a reading, not a disease. Something is pushing it. In Joel's twenty years of ICU and emergency nursing, the same three things did the pushing over and over again. We call them the three pressures. Stress, sugar, sodium. It is nothing mystical. It is a checklist.

Here is the part that almost nobody says out loud. Each of those three pressures is a hormone. Not a mood, not a habit, not a character flaw. A chemical messenger with a name, made in a gland, doing a job it was designed to do. That is why a hormone guide and a blood pressure guide turned out to be the same guide.

Stress is cortisol

Cortisol is made in your adrenal glands, the two small caps sitting on top of your kidneys. In a real emergency it is exactly what you want. It sharpens you up, raises the tone in your blood vessel walls so blood reaches the muscles, and tells your kidneys to hold on to sodium in case you are about to bleed.

The problem is that your body cannot tell the difference between a real emergency and a two in the morning worry about money. It answers both the same way. When cortisol stays high for months and years, the vessels stay a little tighter and the kidneys keep a little more sodium than they need to. Narrower pipes, plus more fluid, equals more pressure. Nothing is broken. The alarm just never got switched off.

Sugar is insulin

You eat fast carbohydrate, blood glucose rises, and insulin pours in to clear it. Insulin's day job is sugar. But insulin has two side effects that matter enormously here.

It signals the kidney to reabsorb more sodium instead of letting it go. And it turns up the sympathetic nervous system, the branch that tightens vessels and speeds the heart. So when insulin runs high for years, which is what insulin resistance means, you get sodium retention and vessel tightening from a hormone most people think has nothing to do with blood pressure at all. This is why some people's readings are worst in the afternoon and they blame the salt on their sandwich.

Sodium is aldosterone

Aldosterone is, quite literally, the body's salt and pressure hormone. It is made in the outer layer of the adrenal gland, and its whole job is to tell the kidney: keep sodium, let go of potassium. Water always follows sodium. More water in a closed system means more pressure inside it.

This is why the sodium corner is not really about the shaker. Your kidney is reading the ratio of potassium to sodium, hour by hour, and setting vessel tone from it. Most people are told to cut sodium and nobody ever tells them the other half, which is that raising potassium from food moves the same ratio. For most people, that half is far easier to actually do.

Cortisol feeds insulin. Insulin feeds aldosterone. The loop keeps turning until you interrupt it somewhere. Food is where most people can interrupt it.

Why licorice is banned from this guide

This is the clearest proof that the three pressures are hormones. Licorice root contains a compound that blocks the enzyme protecting your kidney's aldosterone switch. Block that enzyme, and your stress hormone starts acting like your salt hormone. The result is sodium held, potassium dumped, and pressure pushed up. One herb, one enzyme, both hormones. That is why licorice is a hard no in this guide, and why you should read the label of every herbal blend you own.

The part almost nobody explains after fifty

Why your pressure changed when the rest of you did.

If your readings were fine for forty years and then quietly started climbing, you are not imagining it and you are not being careless. Three more hormone shifts arrive in mid life, and all three touch blood pressure.

For women: menopause

Oestrogen does a job most women are never told about. It helps the lining of your blood vessels produce nitric oxide, the signal that tells a vessel to relax and open. It is one of the quiet reasons women tend to have lower blood pressure than men through the middle decades of life.

When oestrogen falls at menopause, that help falls away. At the same time, arteries naturally stiffen with age. Put those together and blood pressure often begins to rise in the years around and after menopause. Rising pressure after menopause is real, it is common, and it is badly under explained to women. It is not a sign you did something wrong.

The food answer is not to replace the hormone. It is to feed the same pathway from the other end. Beets, leafy greens, arugula, watermelon, pomegranate and garlic all support the nitric oxide signal directly. That is why they are the first things on this list, and why they carry the gold tag.

For men: testosterone and the middle

Testosterone declines slowly from around the forties. As it does, body composition tends to shift, and fat gathers around the middle and around the organs rather than under the skin. That deep visceral fat is not passive storage. It is hormonally active tissue, and it drives insulin resistance.

So the male version of this story usually runs through the sugar corner. More visceral fat, more insulin resistance, more insulin, more sodium held by the kidney, more sympathetic tone. Same destination, different road. The single most useful habit here is the walk after the meal.

For everyone: the thyroid

Your thyroid sets the pace of the whole body, and it moves blood pressure in both directions depending on which way it drifts.

  • An underactive thyroid tends to stiffen the blood vessels and slow the heart. The classic pattern is a bottom number that creeps up while the top number stays put.
  • An overactive thyroid speeds the heart and widens the gap between the two numbers, so the top number climbs while the bottom number falls.

Both are common after fifty, both are easy to test for, and both are missed constantly. If you have never had a thyroid panel, that is a reasonable thing to ask for at your next appointment. The foods on this list that carry the thyroid tag give the gland the iodine and selenium it needs to do its job. They do not replace testing.

The encouraging part

Most hormone chaos is not permanent damage. It is interference. Stress, processed food, blood sugar swings, poor sleep and too little movement are the static between the signal and the cell. Take the interference away and give the body the raw material it needs, and it usually knows exactly what to do. Your body is not broken. It is waiting for a different environment.

Non negotiable

Read this before you change anything.

Food is not neutral once you are on medication. Most of what follows is good news, but a few of these are real, and we would rather you be annoyed by this page than surprised by a lab result.

The one that matters most for this audience

Potassium plus certain blood pressure medications. Half of this guide is potassium rich food, and potassium is exactly what most people need more of. But three families of medication make your body hold potassium instead of clearing it:

  • ACE inhibitors. Names usually ending in "pril". Lisinopril, enalapril, ramipril, benazepril.
  • ARBs. Names usually ending in "sartan". Losartan, valsartan, olmesartan, irbesartan.
  • Potassium sparing diuretics. Spironolactone, eplerenone, triamterene, amiloride.

If you take one of these, or if you have any degree of kidney disease, do not go from almost no potassium to a large amount overnight. Build up gradually over weeks. Tell your prescriber you are adding potassium rich foods. Ask for a potassium level with your next routine bloodwork. That is a normal, easy request and any prescriber will understand it. Too much potassium in the blood is a genuine problem, and it is silent until it is not.

The trap nobody warns people about

Salt substitutes. The shaker labelled "no salt" or "lite salt" is usually potassium chloride. It is a concentrated potassium dose in a shaker, and it does not behave like the potassium in a banana. If you take an ACE inhibitor, an ARB, or a potassium sparing diuretic, ask your prescriber before you use one, every time. Getting potassium from food is safer than getting it from a shaker, and it works.

Licorice root raises blood pressure. It is a hard exclusion.

This one goes the wrong direction, so it is on the avoid list and it is not one of the 101. Glycyrrhizin, the compound in real licorice root, blocks the enzyme that protects your kidney's aldosterone switch. The result is your kidney holding sodium and dumping potassium at the same time, which is the exact opposite of everything else in this guide. It can raise blood pressure and lower potassium together.

Check every herbal blend in your cupboard. Licorice hides in a great many "throat", "digestive" and "detox" blends, in some sweets, and in real black licorice candy. Deglycyrrhizinated licorice, labelled DGL, has had the offending compound removed and is a different thing.

Hawthorn adds to your medication

Hawthorn is the traditional Western heart herb and people ask about it constantly, so here is the straight answer. Hawthorn can potentiate blood pressure medication, meaning it adds to the effect of the pill you are already taking and can take your pressure lower than anyone planned. It also interacts with digoxin. Low blood pressure lands people in the emergency department too.

Do not start hawthorn on your own if you take a blood pressure medication or digoxin. Ask your prescriber first, every time. It appears in this guide, at number 97, with that same warning attached to it.

Say this at your next appointment

"I am adding more potassium rich plant foods and a few kitchen herbs to my routine. I am not changing any of my medications. Can we check a potassium level next time, and is there anything on this list you want me to avoid?" Take the guide with you. It was written to be readable in an exam room.

The other five you should know

Five more, then we get to the food.

Grapefruit, and why it is not one of the 101

Grapefruit blocks an enzyme in your gut that breaks down certain drugs, which can push blood levels of those drugs higher than intended. It matters most with several statins and with some calcium channel blockers, including certain forms of nifedipine and felodipine. Grapefruit is a healthy fruit. It is deliberately left off this list anyway, because we cannot know what is in your pill box. If you love it, ask your prescriber or your pharmacist first. A pharmacist will answer this in one minute and will be glad you asked.

Garlic, ginger and concentrated omega 3, if you take a blood thinner

Garlic, ginger, turmeric, cayenne and high dose plant omega 3 supplements all have a mild antiplatelet effect. In the amounts you use to cook, that is not a concern for most people. If you take warfarin, clopidogrel, apixaban, rivaroxaban or daily aspirin, keep herbs to normal culinary amounts, skip the concentrated capsules unless your prescriber has approved them, and tell your surgeon or your dentist about every herb and supplement you take before any procedure.

Warfarin and leafy greens: consistency, not avoidance

If you take warfarin, the goal is consistency. Greens are high in vitamin K, and warfarin is dosed against your usual vitamin K intake. Swinging from no greens to a mountain of greens is what causes trouble. Pick an amount you can repeat most days, tell your prescriber you are doing it, and let them adjust to you. Do not quietly cut greens out either. Steady is the whole word.

Everyday pain relievers work against you

Ibuprofen, naproxen and the rest of the NSAID family make your kidneys hold sodium and fluid, and they blunt several blood pressure medications. Occasional use for a headache is one conversation. Daily use for arthritis is another, and it is worth raising with your prescriber, because it may be quietly costing you every single day.

Cold and sinus medicines

Pseudoephedrine and phenylephrine constrict blood vessels by design. That is how they clear a blocked nose. Read the label on anything you buy for a cold, and ask a pharmacist for a version that is appropriate with your blood pressure.

Signs to stop and call someone

Dizziness on standing, new lightheadedness, fainting, a reading much lower than your normal, new muscle weakness, a slow or irregular heartbeat, or new swelling. Any of those, stop the new herb and call your prescriber.

Chest pain, trouble breathing, weakness on one side of the body, or a sudden severe headache is not a phone call. That is 911.

Nothing in this guide is a substitute for the judgement of the person who prescribes your medication. The interactions listed here are the common, well documented ones, and no list can be complete for every person or every prescription. Your pharmacist has your full medication list in front of them and will check any of this for free.

Reading a label in ten seconds

Three numbers. Sodium, sugar, fibre. In that order, for this audience.

  • Sodium. Anything at or over 500mg per serving is a lot for one item. Check the serving size before you feel relieved, because it is often half of what you would actually eat.
  • Sugar. More than 6g per serving is worth a second look. Watch for the words ending in "ose", and for syrups, concentrates and anything called "natural cane".
  • Fibre. Under 2g means the grain has been stripped. That is the fastest way to spot a food that will spike your sugar and pull insulin up behind it.
  • Then the ingredients. If oil is in the first three, or there are more than five or six things you do not recognise, put it back.

One habit worth more than the rest of this page

Compare two brands of the same thing, once a week. Bread, tinned tomatoes, plant milk, soup. The gap between the best and worst brand of the same product is usually enormous, and it costs you nothing but ten seconds in the aisle.

How to build a plate without a recipe

Two plate rules, and nine meals under ten minutes.

Plate rule one. Eat heaviest in the morning.

Most people run the day backwards. Light breakfast, huge dinner, then they wonder why they are exhausted at three, craving sugar at four and wide awake at midnight. Your body handles carbohydrate better earlier in the day, and the evening is for repair rather than heavy digestion. Front load it. Heaviest at breakfast, steady at lunch, lightest at night.

Plate rule two. More green than any other colour.

Not a rule about calories. A rule about the ratio your kidney is reading. If green is the largest thing on the plate, the potassium takes care of itself.
